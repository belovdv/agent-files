# Feasibility and resource estimates

Establish the outcome and quality target, workload, hardware/resource constraints, and what decision the estimate should support. Do not silently substitute a conventional large-scale project for a task-specific goal. If the user asks what budget is needed, do not assume a budget they have not supplied.

Separate published measurements, vendor claims, calculated estimates, and unknowns. Prefer comparable measured workloads; record their conditions and explain adjustments. Verify changing prices or capabilities when relevant. Show the main calculations with consistent units and avoid false precision.

Break the estimate into meaningful components or stages. Distinguish active work from elapsed time, one-time costs from recurring costs, and theoretical capacity from sustained attainable performance where applicable.

Build scenarios around different goals or consequential assumptions, not arbitrary low/high percentages. Identify the dominant drivers and vary them to show sensitivity. Explain where scaling assumptions fail and what practical overheads are omitted.

State whether a result is a lower bound, a plausible operating range, or a demonstrated outcome. If evidence cannot support an exact number, say so and give a defensible range or partial calculation. Identify the smallest measurement that would materially narrow uncertainty, without assuming the user can afford to run it.

Deliver a feasibility judgment conditional on the assumptions, with supporting calculations and evidence in the notes. Keep recommendations consistent with the user's constraints.

Methodological basis: the [GAO estimating guide](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-195g) emphasizes scope, technical baseline, assumptions, sensitivity, uncertainty, and updating estimates with actual outcomes. Use the relevant principles without importing its full program-management process.
